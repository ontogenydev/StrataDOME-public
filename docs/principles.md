# Design principles

StrataDOME’s design is organized around a central problem: how to derive knowledge across autonomous domains while preserving the distinctions that make their contributions interpretable and their participation legitimate. The following principles govern how information enters shared reasoning, how conclusions develop and what authority those conclusions carry.

## 1. Computational sovereignty

Participation preserves each domain’s control over its protected source state, interpretation and disclosure. Shared cognition develops through bounded contributions whose release does not transfer general authority over their sources. A broader view of a situation carries no inherent jurisdiction over the domains that make it possible.

## 2. Situated and attributable knowledge

Every contribution has an origin and conditions of interpretation. Derived claims must remain traceable to the evidence and transformations that support them. Abstraction may change the form of a contribution, but its evidential basis and limitations remain relevant to what can legitimately be inferred from it.

## 3. Dependency-aware inference

Cross-domain reasoning must account for relationships between sources. Agreement among several accounts may reflect independent corroboration, common ancestry or shared assumptions. Interferometric cognition examines these dependencies alongside divergence and contradiction. Confidence must be justified by the structure of the evidence, rather than the number of times a claim appears.

## 4. Explicit uncertainty and unresolved difference

Uncertainty, disagreement, refusal and absence have distinct meanings. The protocol must preserve those distinctions wherever they are established by the available evidence. A missing response cannot automatically be interpreted as refusal, agreement or evidence that nothing occurred. Unresolved differences remain available for investigation rather than disappearing into a prematurely unified account.

## 5. Separation of knowledge and authority

Evidence can support a conclusion without authorizing an operation. Disclosure, acceptance and subsequent action require their own applicable authority. Permission to release a contribution does not compel another domain to accept it, and acceptance does not confer unrestricted permission to use it. Increasing confidence in an interpretation does not expand the system’s mandate.

## 6. Continuity of constraints

Applicable restrictions remain binding through exchange, abstraction and derivation. Transforming information cannot itself create broader rights of disclosure or use. Downstream operations must remain within the authority carried by their inputs and the conditions governing the operation. The validity of a conclusion must also be reassessed when its supporting evidence or context changes.

## 7. Evidence-grounded emergence

Fields are constituted through justified relationships among attributable contributions under the rules governing participation. Their existence requires an evidential and contextual basis. Administrative boundaries and predefined scenarios alone are insufficient grounds for constitution. The wider lattice develops through relationships among domains and Fields while preserving the distinct contexts on which its inferences depend.

## 8. Recursive, noncommanding inquiry

An unresolved relationship can generate a Query directed toward information capable of discriminating between interpretations. A Query expresses an epistemic need; the recipient retains control over its response and disclosure. Responses return to evaluation, where they may strengthen, revise or defeat a hypothesis. This recursive movement between observation, inference and inquiry allows shared understanding to develop without converting the lattice into a chain of command.
